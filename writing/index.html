Body Image and the Quiet Weight of Comparison

By Joseph Zou · Summer 2025

The gym has always been part of my routine. It is where I go to train, clear my head, and feel a sense of structure in my day. This past summer, though, something shifted. I was lifting consistently, eating well, and seeing visible progress. On paper, everything was going right. Mentally, it felt heavier than ever.

The more disciplined I became, the harder it was to feel satisfied with my body. Progress did not bring relief. It only raised the standard.

I started noticing how often my attention drifted away from what I was doing and toward how I looked. Between sets, I would catch my reflection in the mirror and focus on what seemed off. My shoulders were not wide enough. My waist looked soft under certain lighting. Even on good days, there was always something to fix.

The Summer It Clicked

That realization hit hardest during the summer of 2025. With more free time, my days revolved around training, recovery, and scrolling. Social media became a constant backdrop, filled with physiques that felt impossibly lean, wide, and defined.

I knew those images were curated and selective, but that knowledge did not stop the comparisons from happening. I would leave the gym feeling strong, then open my phone and feel small again.

What surprised me was how subtle the shift was. There was no single moment where I felt bad about my body. It built quietly through repetition. Each comparison felt harmless on its own, but together they shaped how I saw myself.

When Progress Stopped Feeling Like Progress

Even on days when I hit personal bests, the satisfaction faded quickly. Compliments from others felt temporary, almost irrelevant. I noticed how often I dismissed positive feedback or explained it away.

The gym was still something I loved, but it had also become a place where I felt exposed. Mirrors were everywhere. Comparisons were constant. It was no longer just about training. It was about how I thought I should look while doing it.

Stepping Back

What helped was stepping back and naming the pattern. Not judging it, just recognizing it. I realized my relationship with fitness had shifted from self improvement to self surveillance. I was tracking everything except how I actually felt.

Once I noticed that, I made small changes. I put my phone away more often at the gym. I focused on performance rather than appearance. None of this fixed everything, but it quieted the noise.

Where It Led

Sitting with these thoughts made me curious about whether others felt the same tension. The quiet dissatisfaction. The feeling of doing everything right and still coming up short. That curiosity is what led me to turn this experience into a project.

I designed a survey about gym habits, social media use, and body satisfaction, and began analyzing the responses using machine learning techniques. Reading through the data felt familiar in a way I did not expect. Many people described the same contradictions I had been wrestling with.

If you are interested in the full analysis, including the models, clustering, and results, you can read more about the Body Image and Gym Culture project on my website. This essay captures what started it. The project captures what I learned when I looked at it more closely.

Body Dysmorphia and Gym Culture

By Joseph Zou · March 2025

The gym has always been a place where I feel both inspired and challenged. But for me, it has also been a space where self-doubt creeps in. Like so many others, I have struggled with body dysmorphia—constantly comparing myself to physiques I saw on social media, always feeling like I was one step behind. Even on days when I hit new PRs, I often left the gym frustrated, convinced I was not progressing fast enough.

My personal struggles became the inspiration for a project where I decided to approach body image issues with the mindset of a data scientist. Instead of letting feelings alone dictate how I viewed my progress, I wanted to collect real data. I designed a survey that gathered responses from over 100 people on topics like gym culture, body image, and social media use. I then cleaned and analyzed the data in Python, testing whether patterns I had experienced personally—such as feeling worse after scrolling Instagram—were reflected in the experiences of others.

Personal Motivation

What pushed me toward this project was the endless loop of comparison I felt online. Every time I opened Instagram or TikTok, I was bombarded with images of leaner, stronger, bigger physiques. Even though I trained consistently and tracked my nutrition, I still felt like I was falling behind. Talking with friends at the gym, I realized this feeling was not unique to me. That realization motivated me to turn my curiosity into a structured study.

Survey and Analysis

The survey covered training habits, social media exposure, levels of body satisfaction, and perceptions of gym culture. Once the data was collected, I processed it with Python libraries like Pandas and NumPy, and ran statistical analyses to test correlations. I looked at demographic factors, gym frequency, and time spent online to see which variables aligned most with negative body image.

“The strongest signal was the connection between frequent social media use and lower reported body satisfaction.”

Visualization was a key part of my analysis. I generated charts that highlighted how patterns emerged across different groups. For example, younger respondents and those who followed fitness influencers heavily tended to report higher levels of dissatisfaction, even when they exercised regularly.

Key Results

One of the clearest outcomes was that people who spent more time on fitness-related social media had significantly lower body satisfaction scores. Another key finding was that many gym-goers, even those training for years, still reported struggling with body image. These results resonated deeply with my own experience: the stronger I got, the more I still felt like I was not enough. Seeing my struggles echoed in the data made the project personal in a way no other academic work has been.

Reflection

This project helped me reframe the way I think about my own training. Data allowed me to step outside my emotions and recognize broader patterns in how people feel about their bodies. While numbers will not fix body dysmorphia, they can shed light on the social and cultural forces that make it so widespread. By combining my passion for the gym with my skills in data science, I not only created a project that was academically rigorous but also personally meaningful.

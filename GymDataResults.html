Body Dysmorphia and Gym Culture Study: Results

By Joseph Zou · Spring 2025

Introduction

The gym has been a major part of my life, but with it comes a constant struggle with body dysmorphia. Social media amplified this for me. Endless scrolling of physiques on Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube left me comparing myself against people who were leaner, stronger, and often enhanced. I wanted to know if others felt the same way, so I designed a survey to measure how social media, gym culture, and body image interact. Over 100 responses were collected, and the results shed light on just how common these struggles are.

Hypotheses

  • People who follow more influencers will feel more pressure to gain muscle (followsInfluencersNum vs needsMoreMuscle).
  • Following influencers is associated with more physique comparisons (followsInfluencersNum vs comparesPhysiqueNum).
  • Frequent physique comparisons lower body satisfaction (comparesPhysiqueNum vs bodySatisfaction5).
  • Frequent physique comparisons increase feelings of being too small (comparesPhysiqueNum vs feelsTooSmall).
  • Frequent physique comparisons increase cultural pressure (comparesPhysiqueNum vs feelsCulturePressure).
  • Frequent physique comparisons increase avoidance of posting physique photos (comparesPhysiqueNum vs avoidsPostingDueToPhysique).
  • Frequent physique comparisons increase mirror checking per day (comparesPhysiqueNum vs mirrorChecksPerDay).
  • Lower body satisfaction is associated with avoiding posting physique photos (bodySatisfaction5 vs avoidsPostingDueToPhysique).

Methodology

I designed and deployed a survey with over 100 participants, asking questions about gym habits, social media exposure, body satisfaction, and cultural pressures. The data was processed using Python (Pandas, NumPy), visualized with Matplotlib, and analyzed with correlation tests and hypothesis testing. For each hypothesis, I examined both the visualization and the statistical test (correlation R, p-value) to determine if there was significance.

Results

1. Influencers vs Muscle Pressure

People who follow more influencers tend to feel more pressure to gain muscle.

Statistical Test: r = 0.42, p < 0.01 (moderate positive correlation)

2. Influencers vs Physique Comparisons

Following more influencers is strongly associated with frequent physique comparisons.

Statistical Test: r = 0.56, p < 0.001 (strong positive correlation)

3. Comparisons vs Body Satisfaction

Frequent physique comparisons are linked to lower body satisfaction.

Statistical Test: r = -0.48, p < 0.001 (strong negative correlation)

4. Comparisons vs Feeling Too Small

Comparisons correlate with feeling too small, reinforcing dysmorphic thought patterns.

Statistical Test: r = 0.51, p < 0.001 (strong positive correlation)

5. Comparisons vs Cultural Pressure

Those who compare physiques more often also report stronger cultural pressure to look a certain way.

Statistical Test: r = 0.45, p < 0.001

6. Comparisons vs Avoiding Posting

Frequent comparisons increase avoidance of posting physique pictures online.

Statistical Test: r = 0.47, p < 0.001

7. Comparisons vs Mirror Checks

More comparisons are tied to more daily mirror checks, suggesting obsessive behavior.

Statistical Test: r = 0.50, p < 0.001

8. Body Satisfaction vs Avoiding Posting

Lower body satisfaction correlates with avoiding physique posts.

Statistical Test: r = -0.39, p < 0.01

Discussion

These results confirm much of what I suspected. Social media exposure, especially following fitness influencers, amplifies pressure, comparisons, and dysmorphic thoughts. Frequent comparisons were tied to nearly every negative indicator, from mirror checking to avoiding social media posts. The strongest effects came from comparisons reducing satisfaction and increasing feelings of being too small. This reflects my own experience: no matter how much progress I make, comparisons often make me feel behind.

Conclusion

This study shows that gym culture and social media create a cycle that drives body dysmorphia. Following influencers and comparing physiques are powerful predictors of dissatisfaction and unhealthy behaviors. Moving forward, I hope to expand this research with a larger dataset, more diverse demographics, and perhaps intervention studies. The results highlight that we need more awareness around the mental health costs of fitness culture.

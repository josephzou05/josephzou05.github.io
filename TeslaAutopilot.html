Tesla and the Ethics of Autonomous Driving

By Joseph Zou · May 2, 2025

The rise of artificial intelligence has ushered in a wave of transformative innovation, but it has also surfaced complex ethical questions. A particularly important case has emerged in recent years surrounding Tesla, the electric vehicle manufacturer led by CEO Elon Musk. The company has faced mounting legal and regulatory scrutiny for how it has promoted and released its Autopilot and Full Self-Driving (FSD) systems.

Background

Tesla marketed Autopilot and FSD as groundbreaking features, often implying full autonomy. Lawsuits and investigations argue the company exaggerated capabilities, contributing to fatal accidents and public confusion. Disclaimers existed, but were often buried in fine print and contradicted by marketing language such as “Autopilot” and “Full Self-Driving.”

A high-profile case involved Genesis Giovanni Mendoza-Martinez, who died in 2023 when his Tesla Model S collided with a parked fire truck while Autopilot was active. His family alleges Tesla’s long-term marketing campaign persuaded consumers to believe the cars could safely drive themselves (Kolodny, 2024; Vigliarolo, 2024).

The Ethical Tradeoff

Tesla argues its technology reduces accidents and saves lives long-term, citing data that Autopilot vehicles crash less per mile than human-driven cars. From Tesla’s perspective, aggressively promoting and deploying these features enables real-world testing, speeds innovation, and serves the harm principle by preventing more accidents overall.

Yet critics argue the short-term risks were foreseeable and preventable. The dilemma is whether Tesla’s pursuit of innovation justified misleading consumers and exposing them to unnecessary harm.

“Tesla is ethically responsible for promoting and releasing its Autopilot and Full Self-Driving systems in misleading ways because it violated the principle of autonomy and failed to uphold the principle of harm.”

Autonomy

Autonomy requires individuals have the information needed for informed, voluntary decisions. Tesla’s branding encouraged drivers to overestimate system capabilities. In-vehicle design choices — subtle icons instead of clear warnings, allowing activation on risky roads — compounded this. Even if drivers technically agreed to disclaimers, they were not adequately informed, undermining true autonomy.

The Mendoza case illustrates this clearly: despite keeping hands on the wheel as instructed, he trusted Autopilot more than was warranted due to Tesla’s long-term advertising campaign. Consumers cannot exercise real autonomy if the information they receive is misleading.

Harm

The harm principle holds that companies must prevent unnecessary and foreseeable harm. Tesla’s rollout of Autopilot and FSD, while exaggerating safety and autonomy, created preventable dangers. The California DMV accused Tesla of false advertising, with a judge ruling the claims could justify enforcement (Stempel, 2024). This confirms the risks were not only foreseeable but officially recognized.

Tesla’s reliance on over-the-air updates rather than recalls after safety investigations reflects a reactive rather than preventative approach, prioritizing rollout over safety. These choices endangered not only drivers but also pedestrians, first responders, and other road users.

Conclusion

Tesla’s Autopilot and FSD systems represent both innovation and ethical failure. By misleading consumers about the technology’s capabilities, Tesla violated autonomy. By releasing incomplete systems that contributed to preventable accidents, it violated the harm principle. Innovation must not come at the cost of human dignity and safety.

Tesla and the Ethics of Autonomous Driving

By Joseph Zou · May 2, 2025

Tesla has faced growing scrutiny for how it promoted and released its Autopilot and Full Self-Driving (FSD) systems. The company claims these features improve safety, but critics argue its marketing overstated their abilities, misleading consumers and contributing to fatal accidents.

Background

Tesla branded its driver-assist systems as “Autopilot” and “Full Self-Driving,” names that suggested full autonomy. Elon Musk repeatedly claimed Teslas would soon operate without human supervision, and promotional videos showed cars navigating traffic with minimal input. Lawsuits argue this messaging created public confusion despite disclaimers buried in fine print.

A notable case involved Genesis Giovanni Mendoza-Martinez, who died in 2023 when his Tesla, on Autopilot, struck a parked fire truck. His family alleged Tesla’s advertising gave him a false sense of security in the system (Kolodny, 2024; Vigliarolo, 2024). Regulators like the California DMV also accused Tesla of false advertising, noting its vehicles could not function as autonomous cars (Stempel, 2024).

The Ethical Dilemma

Tesla argues that rapid deployment accelerates real-world testing, speeds improvements, and reduces accidents over time. From this perspective, aggressively promoting Autopilot and FSD aligns with the harm principle by improving long-term safety. As a company competing in a fast-moving industry, Tesla might also defend its strategy as necessary for growth.

Yet these benefits came at the cost of foreseeable risks. Marketing incomplete systems as nearly autonomous shifted preventable dangers onto drivers and the public. The dilemma is whether Tesla’s push for innovation justified misleading consumers and exposing them to unnecessary harm.

“Tesla is ethically responsible for promoting and releasing its Autopilot and Full Self-Driving systems in misleading ways because it violated autonomy and failed to uphold the principle of harm.”

Autonomy

Autonomy requires that people have the information needed to make informed choices. Tesla’s branding encouraged drivers to overestimate system abilities, and its interface reinforced this by using subtle icons instead of clear warnings. Even when drivers like Mendoza followed instructions, they misunderstood the system’s true limitations. Without transparency, meaningful consent was impossible.

Harm

The harm principle obligates companies to prevent foreseeable injury. Tesla promoted Autopilot and FSD as safer and more autonomous than they were, contributing to preventable crashes. The California DMV’s accusations confirm that risks were foreseeable and misleading (Stempel, 2024). Tesla’s reliance on over-the-air updates rather than recalls showed a reactive approach that prioritized rollout over safety, endangering not only Tesla drivers but also other road users.

Conclusion

Tesla’s Autopilot and FSD reflect both innovation and ethical failure. By misrepresenting capabilities, Tesla violated autonomy. By releasing systems that contributed to preventable harm, it violated the harm principle. Progress in autonomous driving must be pursued with transparency and responsibility to protect users and the public.

Tesla and the Ethics of Autonomous Driving

By Joseph Zou · May 2, 2025

Tesla has revolutionized electric vehicles, but its promotion of Autopilot and Full Self-Driving (FSD) systems has raised serious ethical concerns. Lawsuits and investigations allege that Tesla overstated the capabilities of these features, leading consumers to believe their vehicles were more autonomous than they truly were. This gap between marketing and reality has contributed to preventable accidents, injuries, and public misunderstanding.

Background

Tesla introduced Autopilot and FSD as driver-assist technologies but branded them with terms implying full autonomy. Elon Musk repeatedly claimed Teslas would soon drive themselves, while company videos showcased cars navigating traffic with minimal human input. Critics argue these messages contradicted disclaimers buried in fine print. A tragic 2023 case illustrates the issue: Genesis Giovanni Mendoza-Martinez died when his Tesla, on Autopilot, collided with a parked fire truck. His family claims Tesla’s long-term advertising campaign persuaded him the vehicle could safely operate independently (Kolodny, 2024; Vigliarolo, 2024).

The Ethical Tradeoff

Tesla argues Autopilot reduces crashes per mile and that rapid deployment accelerates improvements, serving the harm principle by reducing accidents long term. Yet the short-term risks were foreseeable. By overselling autonomy, Tesla encouraged overreliance on an unfinished system, shifting preventable risks onto consumers.

“Tesla is ethically responsible for promoting and releasing Autopilot and Full Self-Driving in misleading ways because it violated autonomy and failed to uphold the principle of harm.”

Autonomy

Autonomy requires that individuals make informed, voluntary choices. Tesla’s branding—“Autopilot,” “Full Self-Driving”—misrepresented limitations. Its interface compounded this by using subtle alerts instead of clear warnings. Many drivers, including Mendoza, followed instructions but still misunderstood the system’s true capabilities. Without transparency, meaningful consent was impossible.

Harm

The harm principle obligates companies to prevent foreseeable harm. Tesla’s exaggerated marketing and rapid rollout led to preventable crashes. Regulators like the California DMV accused Tesla of false advertising, and investigations showed Autopilot failed in emergency scenarios (Stempel, 2024). Over-the-air updates replaced recalls, reflecting a reactive rather than preventative safety strategy. These choices endangered not only drivers but also pedestrians and first responders.

Conclusion

Tesla’s Autopilot and FSD embody both innovation and ethical failure. By misleading consumers, Tesla violated autonomy. By releasing systems known to cause preventable accidents, it violated the harm principle. Progress in autonomous driving must be balanced with responsibility to minimize risk and respect user autonomy.

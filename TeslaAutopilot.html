Tesla and the Ethics of Autonomous Driving

By Joseph Zou · May 2, 2025

Tesla has faced mounting scrutiny for how it promoted and released its Autopilot and Full Self-Driving (FSD) systems. While Tesla claims these features improve safety, critics argue the company overstated their capabilities, contributing to fatal accidents and public confusion. Lawsuits and investigations suggest Tesla misled the public by giving the impression its cars could operate autonomously, even though human supervision was required.

Background

Tesla aggressively marketed Autopilot and FSD with terms implying full autonomy. Elon Musk often stated the cars would soon drive themselves, and promotional videos showed vehicles navigating traffic with minimal input. These claims were contradicted by incidents where Autopilot was active during crashes, including a fatal 2023 collision involving Genesis Giovanni Mendoza-Martinez. Lawsuits argue Tesla’s long-term advertising campaign persuaded the public that its vehicles were capable of self-driving when they were not (Vigliarolo, 2024).

In 2022, the California DMV accused Tesla of false advertising, noting that its vehicles “could not at the time of those advertisements, and cannot now, operate as autonomous vehicles” (Stempel, 2024). This official recognition underscores the ethical issues at stake.

The Ethical Tradeoff

Tesla frames Autopilot as advancing innovation and public safety by reducing accidents and fatigue. The company argues that wide deployment accelerates real-world testing, improving long-term safety. Tesla also faces pressure to innovate in a competitive industry. However, these potential benefits must be weighed against the risk of misleading consumers and exposing them to preventable harm in the short term.

“Tesla is ethically responsible for promoting Autopilot and FSD in misleading ways because it violated autonomy and failed to uphold the principle of harm.”

Autonomy

By branding its features “Autopilot” and “Full Self-Driving,” Tesla encouraged drivers to believe the cars were more autonomous than they were. Musk’s public statements and Tesla’s design choices reinforced this confusion. Disclaimers requiring driver attention were buried in fine print and contradicted by messaging and interfaces that implied safety and independence. This denied consumers the information needed to make informed decisions.

The Mendoza-Martinez case illustrates the problem. Despite following Tesla’s instructions to keep hands on the wheel, he believed Autopilot could safely handle highway driving. This overreliance shows how misleading marketing and design undermined consumer autonomy.

Harm

The principle of harm requires avoiding foreseeable, preventable risks. Tesla promoted Autopilot and FSD as safer and more capable than they were, even as federal and state agencies documented their limits. By releasing systems with known weaknesses, Tesla created a foreseeable risk of misuse and accidents. Following crashes, Tesla issued software updates instead of recalls, reinforcing a reactive rather than preventative approach.

Beyond drivers, Tesla’s deployment affected pedestrians, first responders, and other road users. Choosing rapid rollout over stronger safeguards placed the public at risk and conflicted with its ethical responsibility to prevent harm.

Conclusion

Tesla’s promotion and release of Autopilot and FSD violated autonomy by failing to give consumers accurate information and violated harm by exposing them to preventable dangers. While innovation in autonomous driving has value, it must not come at the expense of transparency and safety. Tesla and other companies must ensure progress is paired with respect for human dignity and risk prevention.
